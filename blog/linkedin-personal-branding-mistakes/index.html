LinkedIn personal branding mistakes B2B founders make, and how to fix each

The costliest LinkedIn personal branding mistakes are posting without a named buyer, chasing reach over recall, sharing generic insight, and leaving the profile unfinished. Each weakens the trust a buyer forms before any conversation starts. This guide sets out eight mistakes, what each looks like on a profile, and the fix.

By Josh Huggins · October 2026 · 8 min read

LinkedIn personal branding mistakes are rarely dramatic. Most are small habits repeated week after week, and a B2B founder can post every week and still lose ground if the profile is unfinished, the content could belong to anyone, or nobody can tell who it is meant for.

This guide is for founders, consultants and senior leaders who sell high-value services and want LinkedIn to support how they win work.

Why these mistakes cost more in B2B

Buyers form a view early. Bain & Company's June 2026 article Raising the Odds on a Deal reports that around 90% of buyers purchase from their Day 1 list, citing earlier research rather than its own survey. Bain's own work with LinkedIn, a February 2026 survey of 750 buyers across two enterprise software categories, found that hidden buyers hold half of the influence over that decision.

Timing makes this harder. LinkedIn's B2B Institute, working with Professor John Dawes of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, estimated in 2021 that around 95% of B2B buyers are not in market at any given time. Most people who read your posts will not need you for months, so what counts is whether they remember you when they do.

If the shortlist is largely set before a seller is contacted, your LinkedIn presence is part of how you reach it. Each mistake below makes that harder.

The eight mistakes in detail

1. Posting without a named buyer

Content written for "my network" lands with nobody in particular. Pick one buyer: the job title, the sector and the problem that occupies them. Write each post to answer a question that person asks before they hire.

A B2B founder selling to finance directors posts differently from one selling to CTOs, and the profile should make that obvious within seconds.

2. Writing for reach instead of recall

A post that 50,000 strangers like achieves little if none of them buy what you sell. The aim is for the right few hundred people to remember your name when a need appears. The Day 1 list and the 95% out-of-market estimate point the same way: most of the buyers who matter are not looking yet, so the job is to be a name they already know when they start.

Track who views your profile and who replies. A reply from a target account tells you more than a run of likes from peers.

3. Sharing insight anyone could have written

Edelman and LinkedIn surveyed 1,300 business decision-makers and C-suite executives in October and November 2016, for a study published in 2017. In the published findings, 56% said that most of the time they gain no valuable insights from the thought leadership they consume, and 45% of decision-makers said poor-quality material made them lose respect for the organisation behind it. The survey is a decade old, so read the figures as direction rather than a current benchmark.

Advice any competitor could have posted gives a buyer no reason to prefer you. The remedy is specificity: put a number, a decision you made, a situation you handled or an opinion you will defend into every post.

4. Leaving the profile unfinished

Every post sends readers to your profile, so it works as your landing page. Check three things: the headline says who you help and what changes for them, the About section opens with the buyer's problem, and the Featured section holds one piece of proof or one clear offer.

Our guide to optimising your LinkedIn profile for B2B clients covers the order in which to fix these.

5. Writing only for the decision-maker

B2B purchases involve a group. The 2025 Edelman-LinkedIn B2B Thought Leadership Impact Report, which draws on nearly 2,000 professionals, cites Matt Dixon's research in The JOLT Effect (2022) that more than 40% of B2B deals stall because of internal misalignment within buying groups.

Content that helps a finance lead, an operations lead and an end user explain your value to colleagues gives the deal somewhere to go. Our post on LinkedIn content for a B2B buying committee shows how to plan it.

6. Posting in bursts, then going quiet

Three posts in a week followed by a month of silence leaves no pattern for people to recognise. The cause is usually the same: writing is the first task dropped when client work gets busy.

7. Hiding behind the company page

Buyers judge the person they will work with. A company page has no face and no first-person voice, and nobody to reply to a comment as themselves. Keep the company page accurate and use it for announcements, and put your views on your own profile.

8. Pitching the moment someone connects

Someone who has just accepted your request has agreed to know who you are, and a pitch in the first message ends the conversation before it starts. Reply with something specific to their work and let your posts do the introducing. Our connection message examples show the tone that gets responses.

How to audit your own LinkedIn personal brand in 30 minutes

Open your profile in a private window and answer these questions as a stranger would.

  1. Can you tell from the headline who you help and what changes for them?
  2. Does your last post name a buyer, a problem and a specific point of view?
  3. Have you posted in each of the last eight weeks?
  4. Would a finance lead or an operations lead find anything in your last five posts that helps them?
  5. Does your Featured section give a reader one clear next step?

Every "no" maps to a mistake above. Fix the profile first, because it affects every post you publish afterwards. Then fix the habit that feeds the posts. Independent consultants and fractional executives often find the answer to question three is the weak point, because client work comes first.
