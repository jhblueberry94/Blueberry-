Day One shortlist: how B2B buyers choose vendors before they call you

A Day One shortlist is the small set of vendors a B2B buyer already has in mind when a buying process starts. Research cited by Bain & Company suggests around nine in ten buyers end up purchasing from that list, so most deals are won or lost before a seller is contacted.

By Josh Huggins · November 2026 · 6 min read

Most B2B companies measure their pipeline from the first enquiry. By then the buyer has usually decided who is in the running. The Day One shortlist is the name for that early list, and it explains why some firms win work without chasing it while others lose deals they never knew existed.

This guide covers what the Day One shortlist is, what the research says about it, and what you can do to be on it. We are a LinkedIn personal branding agency, so our angle is visibility on LinkedIn, and we link to the research behind each figure.

What the Day One shortlist is

Bain & Company describes the Day 1 list as the vendors a buyer has in mind at the beginning of their buying process. It forms before anyone fills in a contact form or books a demo, from what the buyer already knows: past experience, recommendations from peers, what they have read, and who they have seen talking sensibly about the problem.

Bain does not put a number on how long the list is. At any length the effect is the same: a vendor missing at the start has to displace someone the buyer already expects to consider.

What the research says about the Day One shortlist

~90%of buyers purchase from their Day 1 list, as cited by Bain & Company, June 2026
50%of the influence over a B2B decision sits with hidden buyers, the people who shape it without leading it. LinkedIn and Bain analysis, cited by Bain & Company, June 2026
13 + 9internal stakeholders and external influencers in a typical buying decision. Forrester, January 2026

Bain puts the consequence plainly: "Most seemingly 'lost deals' were never winnable," because the vendor was missing from the buyer's first list.

Taken together, the figures show the decision is usually made early by a group, working from a list the vendor did not write.

How B2B buyers build the list

Most of the shortlist forms out of sight. It draws on four things:

  • Reputation. The names a buyer already links with the problem.
  • Peers. Recommendations from colleagues inside the buying group and from the external influencers Forrester counts.
  • Independent research. Search engines, AI tools, review sites and LinkedIn. In G2's 2026 survey, review sites and AI chatbots were the two biggest influences on buyers' shortlists.
  • What hidden buyers have read. Edelman and LinkedIn's 2025 research found that 71% of hidden buyers have little or no interaction with sales teams, so what they know about you comes from what you publish and what others say about you.

LinkedIn matters here because it is where B2B buyers can see the people behind a company. A buyer who has read a founder's posts for six months already has a view on whether that founder understands the problem. We look at this research behaviour in more depth in B2B buying behaviour on LinkedIn.

How to get on the Day One shortlist

  1. Be known for a specific problem. Buyers remember the person who explained their problem clearly. Pick the one or two topics where your experience is deepest and keep returning to them.
  2. Show up before the need exists. The list forms when a project starts, so your visibility has to come first. Publishing consistently on LinkedIn means buyers meet your thinking months before they go looking.
  3. Speak to the whole buying group. Write for the finance lead, the operational lead and the end user as well as the person who signs. Our guide to LinkedIn content for a B2B buying committee covers this.
  4. Make your experts visible. Buyers trust people they can see. Founders, partners and senior specialists posting under their own names give buyers someone to judge, in a way a company page cannot.
  5. Earn mentions you do not control. Bain recommends activating social proof through customer validation, partner advocacy, references and third-party endorsement. Podcasts, guest articles and client referrals all count.
  6. Stay easy to check. A clear LinkedIn profile, a website that says plainly what you do, and straight answers to common questions help human buyers and the AI tools many now use for research.

How to tell whether you are making the shortlist

Few companies measure this. Wynter's survey of 100 marketing leaders at B2B SaaS companies with more than $50 million in revenue, reported in Inside day-1 shortlists, found that half do not track whether they make buyers' shortlists. Four simple checks help:

  • Ask every new prospect who else they are considering and how they first heard of you.
  • Count how many enquiries name a person, such as the founder, rather than the company.
  • Look at who views your profile and engages with your posts, and whether they work at target accounts.
  • Track how often you are invited to pitch without having approached the buyer first.
