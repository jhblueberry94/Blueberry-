LinkedIn follow vs connect: which to use as a B2B leader

Connecting creates a two-way relationship that opens free messaging, while following is a one-way subscription to someone's public posts. For B2B leaders, follow people you want to be known by, connect once there has been a real exchange, and choose the main button on your own profile by whether you want audience or network.

By Josh Huggins · September 2026 · 7 min read

The LinkedIn follow vs connect choice decides how strangers meet you and how you meet them. A connection request asks the other person to agree to a relationship, while a follow needs no agreement at all. That difference shapes who sees your content, who can message you and whether a first conversation feels warm or cold.

This guide explains what each one does, compares them side by side, and sets out when a B2B founder, consultant or sales leader should use each. It also covers the decision most people miss: whether your own profile should invite people to follow you or to connect.

What following and connecting each do on LinkedIn

Connecting is mutual. One person sends a request, the other accepts, and both become first-degree connections. From then on each can message the other for free and each sees the other's posts. LinkedIn caps first-degree connections at 30,000.

Following is one-way. You see someone's public posts in your feed, they are not added to your network, and you cannot message them directly unless their settings allow it or you share a connection. The number of followers you can have is unlimited. Sending a connection request usually follows the person as well, and a request that stays pending or is declined often leaves you following them.

LinkedIn adjusts these behaviours and limits from time to time, so check the current rules in LinkedIn's help centre before building a process around a number.

When to follow first

Follow first when the person does not know you yet: a target buyer, a possible referrer, or a leader whose audience you want to join. A follow costs them nothing and asks for nothing, which is exactly why it works as a first step.

It matters because of how B2B buying works. The 6sense 2025 B2B Buyer Report found that buyers complete around 61% of their journey before first contact with a vendor, and that buyers initiate around 79% of the contact with sellers. When the buyer makes the first move, the seller's job beforehand is to be a name that buyer already recognises.

Following only works if you then take part. Comment on their posts in a way that adds a view or a fact, and let them see your name a few times before you ask for anything. A follow with no engagement behind it is invisible to the other person.

When to connect

Connect when there is already something between you: a call, a meeting, an introduction, an exchange in the comments or a shared event. In those cases a request confirms an existing relationship and is accepted for that reason.

Connect straight away with clients, peers and referral partners, because you want messaging open and the relationship visible to both networks. Add a one-line note that says where you met or what you discussed. Our connection message examples show what tends to get accepted.

Gartner research shows that B2B buyers spend only 17% of their purchase time in direct contact with vendors. Sales leaders who wait for a buyer to enter that window before making contact are asking for attention at the busiest moment. Should your own profile say follow or connect?

LinkedIn lets you choose whether the main button on your profile invites people to follow you or to connect with you. The setting sits in your profile and privacy settings, and LinkedIn moves it around, so check the current location rather than relying on an old screenshot.

If you publish regularly and want strangers to see your posts without asking permission, Follow as the main button lowers the barrier. A stranger can subscribe in one click, and you never have to decide whether to accept. That suits a B2B founder building an audience.

If your work runs on referrals and known relationships, Connect keeps your network tighter and your inbox cleaner. Many independent consultants prefer it for that reason. A common setup for leaders who post consistently is Follow as the main button, with connections made deliberately after a real exchange.

A simple sequence that uses both

For a prospect you would like to work with, follow them, comment usefully on a few of their posts over a couple of weeks, and then send a connection request with a note that refers to something they said. Once they accept, keep the conversation about their work rather than yours.

This is the logic behind our LinkedIn outreach and growth service: visibility first, request second. A prospect who has already seen your posts and your comments treats a connection request as coming from a familiar name and has context that a cold request lacks. A sales leader running outreach this way builds pipeline from relationships rather than from volume.

Common mistakes with follow and connect

The first mistake is sending connection requests with no note and no visible activity on your profile. The recipient sees a stranger with nothing to judge, and the request is easy to ignore.

The second is following without ever taking part. A follow only helps if the other person eventually sees your name attached to something useful.

The third is treating connection count as the goal. Connections are capped and followers are not, so the right measure is whether the people in your network are the ones you want to work with.

The fourth is pitching the moment a request is accepted. Acceptance opens the door to a conversation, and an immediate sales message closes it again.

The fifth is neglecting your own profile. Someone who follows you sees your profile and posts before anything else, so it needs to work for a stranger. Our guide to optimising your LinkedIn profile for B2B clients covers what to fix first, and the wider Blueberry Media blog has more on outreach.
