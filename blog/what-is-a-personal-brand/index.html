What is a personal brand? A guide for B2B leaders on LinkedIn

A personal brand is what people believe about you, your expertise and your judgement before they have spoken to you. For B2B leaders it is built mostly on LinkedIn, through what you publish and how you show up, and it shapes whether buyers trust you enough to put you on their shortlist.

By Josh Huggins · November 2026 · 7 min read

If you have ever looked someone up on LinkedIn before a meeting, you have already judged a personal brand. A personal brand is the picture people form of you from what they can see: what you say, what you share and how consistently you say it. This guide explains what a personal brand is, how it differs from personal branding, and how B2B founders and leaders build one on LinkedIn.

We are a LinkedIn personal branding agency, so we have a view on this. Where a point rests on research, we link to the source.

What a personal brand means in practice

Everyone in business has a personal brand, whether they work on it or not. It is your reputation, made visible to people who have not met you yet.

For a B2B leader, that reputation usually answers the questions a buyer has before they get in touch:

  • What do you know that I need?
  • Do you understand problems like mine?
  • Would I trust your judgement with my budget?

If your LinkedIn profile and posts answer those questions clearly, people arrive at a first call already leaning towards you. If they answer nothing, buyers fill the gap with your job title and whatever your company page says.

Personal brand vs personal branding

The two terms get used interchangeably, but they describe different things. Your personal brand is the result: what people think of you. Personal branding is the work you do to shape that result, such as choosing what to be known for, writing posts and keeping your profile current.

What it means to be the CEO of your personal brand

The phrase goes back to Tom Peters' 1997 Fast Company article, The Brand Called You. Peters wrote that "today, in the Age of the Individual, you have to be your own brand," and urged readers to run their careers as if they were the CEO of their own company.

In practice, being the CEO of your personal brand means making the calls a CEO would make: what you stand for, who you serve, and what you will leave alone. On LinkedIn it also means owning everything published in your name, even when someone helps you write it.

Why a personal brand matters for B2B leaders

B2B buyers do most of their research before they speak to anyone, and many of the people in a buying group never speak to a vendor at all. Edelman and LinkedIn's 2025 B2B Thought Leadership Impact Report, based on nearly 2,000 professionals, looked at these "hidden buyers": people who influence a purchase without leading it.

71%of hidden buyers have little or no interaction with sales teams. Edelman-LinkedIn, 2025
53%say strong content can outweigh brand recognition alone. Edelman-LinkedIn, 2025
35%say a senior executive encouraged them to consider a vendor because of its thought leadership. Edelman-LinkedIn, 2025

Those hidden buyers still read what leaders publish: the same report found 63% of them spend more than an hour a week on thought leadership content. A buyer who already knows how you think is easier to talk to, and a leader with a visible point of view is easier to recommend to colleagues. We go further into how buyers research before contact in B2B buying behaviour on LinkedIn.

The same logic applies whether you are a B2B founder, a consultant or a fractional executive. In each case, people are buying your judgement, and your personal brand is how they get a look at it first.

What a strong personal brand looks like on LinkedIn

A well-built personal brand is easy to sum up in one sentence. Here are three composite examples, based on the kinds of leaders we work with rather than real people:

  • A fractional CFO known for getting scale-ups ready for their first audit, who shares lessons from finance clean-ups every week.
  • A cybersecurity founder known for explaining new regulations in plain English for board members.
  • A management consultant known for one clear view on why change programmes stall, backed by stories from real projects.

Each one is specific about the topic and the audience, and none of them depends on a large following. What they share is consistency: the same subject, from the same person, often enough that buyers remember it when the need comes up.

Sum it up in a personal brand statement

A personal brand statement is that one sentence, written down. It says who you help, with what, and where your angle comes from. A simple version: "I help [who] with [problem], drawing on [your experience or point of view]."

For the fractional CFO above, it might read: "I help scale-up founders get audit-ready without slowing growth, drawing on a decade of finance clean-ups." Put it near the top of your profile and use it to decide what to post. If a post idea doesn't fit the statement, it probably belongs somewhere else.

How to build a personal brand on LinkedIn

  1. Decide what you want to be known for, and by whom. Pick one or two topics where your experience runs deep and your buyers care about the outcome, then write your personal brand statement.
  2. Make your profile match. Your headline, About section and featured posts should say what your posts say. Our guide to LinkedIn profile optimisation for B2B clients covers this.
  3. Publish from your own experience. Stories from client work, decisions you made and things you got wrong are harder to copy than general advice.
  4. Show up in other people's conversations. Useful comments on posts your buyers read keep you in view between your own posts.
  5. Judge it by conversations. Profile views from the right people, inbound messages and warmer first calls tell you more than likes.
