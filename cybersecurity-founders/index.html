Why cybersecurity buyers are hard to reach

The UK cyber security sector is crowded and growing. The Department for Science, Innovation and Technology's Cyber Security Sectoral Analysis 2026, published on 12 May 2026, counts 2,603 UK cyber security firms, up 20% from 2,165 the year before, with 438 of them new. Revenue reached £14.7 billion, up 11%. A buyer comparing vendors meets many similar claims.

Buyers respond by leaning on people they trust. NOLA Marketing and the Ponemon Institute surveyed 320 enterprise cybersecurity decision-makers for The State of Cybersecurity Marketing Influence 2026 (June 2026). 55% ranked peer recommendations as their top discovery channel, and 46% said research and survey reports have the most direct impact on selection. 52% said vendor messaging lacks technical depth, and 49% said it gives insufficient ROI justification.

Peers recommend people whose thinking they can repeat, so the job of your content is to be worth repeating.

How a founder's LinkedIn presence works as peer evidence

A peer recommendation usually sounds like "they explain this well". A security leader who has seen your explanations of a threat, a standard or a trade-off in their feed over several months can pass that on in one sentence, and specific posts give them the material.

Research carries weight too. If your team sees patterns across engagements, a short series of posts built on anonymised, aggregated findings, with the method stated, works like a small research report and sits where buyers already read.

What good LinkedIn content looks like for a cybersecurity founder

These five angles suit cybersecurity founders and leaders:

  • A threat or incident type explained for a non-technical board, with no client or vulnerability identified.
  • A framework or standard translated into the decisions it forces, such as Cyber Essentials or ISO 27001.
  • A pattern from your own work, aggregated and anonymised, with the method stated.
  • A technical trade-off: why you detect, respond or architect one way, and what it costs.
  • Plain ROI reasoning: what a buyer can reasonably expect to measure, and what they cannot.

Confidentiality is built in. Nothing that could identify a client or an unpatched weakness is published unless you have cleared it.
